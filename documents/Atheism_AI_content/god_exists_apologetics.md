# Phase 3 — God Exists

## A Philosophical and Apologetic Case for the Existence of God

### Purpose

This phase establishes that belief in God is philosophically rational before using the Resurrection as evidence for God's action in history.

The central question is:

> **Why does anything exist at all, and what ultimately explains the existence, order, intelligibility, consciousness, and moral dimension of reality?**

The goal is not to prove every attribute of the Christian God in one argument.

Instead, establish progressively:

> **Something exists.**  
> ↓  
> **Contingent reality requires an ultimate explanation.**  
> ↓  
> **An ultimate explanation cannot itself be merely another contingent thing.**  
> ↓  
> **A necessary, transcendent foundation of reality is rationally justified.**  
> ↓  
> **Further arguments can establish attributes such as unity, immateriality, eternity, intelligence, power, and goodness.**  
> ↓  
> **This gives us philosophical grounds for theism.**

The later Resurrection argument then asks:

> **Has this God acted in history by raising Jesus from the dead?**

---

# 1. What Exactly Are We Trying to Prove?

Avoid beginning with:

> "The Catholic God exists."

That is too much for this stage.

The first target is:

> **There exists a necessary, transcendent foundation of contingent reality.**

From there, we can investigate what properties such a foundation must possess.

A useful distinction:

### Minimal conclusion

> There is a necessary ground of contingent reality.

### Stronger conclusion

> The necessary ground is transcendent, immaterial, eternal, powerful, and intelligent.

### Theistic conclusion

> A personal/intelligent necessary being—God—exists.

### Christian conclusion

> This God has revealed Himself in history.

The final step belongs to later phases.

---

# 2. Important Philosophical Distinctions

## Necessary vs. Contingent

A **contingent** thing is something that could have failed to exist.

Examples:

- a human being;
- a tree;
- a planet;
- a particular star;
- a computer;
- the Earth;
- the observable universe, if considered as a contingent physical reality.

A **necessary** reality cannot fail to exist.

It does not depend upon something else for its existence.

---

# 3. The Principle of Sufficient Reason

A major philosophical principle is:

> **Things that exist have an adequate explanation for why they exist rather than not.**

This does not necessarily mean:

> "Every event has a physical cause."

That would be too narrow.

The question is broader:

> **Why is there something rather than nothing?**

A contingent object may be explained by another contingent object.

But if the entire collection of contingent reality is itself contingent, we still need to ask:

> **Why does the contingent order exist at all?**

---

# 4. Argument from Contingency

This is one of the strongest classical routes.

### Premise 1

Contingent things exist.

We know this from ordinary experience.

### Premise 2

Contingent things do not contain the complete reason for their own existence.

A contingent thing could have failed to exist.

### Premise 3

The existence of contingent reality requires an explanation.

### Premise 4

The ultimate explanation cannot itself be merely another contingent thing.

Otherwise we have not reached an ultimate explanation.

### Conclusion

> **There exists a necessary reality that grounds contingent existence.**

This is the basic contingency argument.

---

# 5. The "Who Created God?" Objection

A common objection is:

> "If God created the universe, who created God?"

The answer is:

> **The argument does not claim that everything needs a creator.**

It claims that contingent things require an explanation.

God, in classical theism, is not proposed as a contingent object.

God is proposed as:

> **necessary being.**

If God required another cause of His existence, then God would not be the ultimate explanation.

The question therefore becomes:

> **Why should we believe God is necessary rather than contingent?**

That requires further argument.

---

# 6. The Universe Cannot Simply Be the Necessary Thing Without Argument

A skeptic may say:

> "Why can't the universe itself be necessary?"

This is a legitimate question.

The response should not simply be:

> "Because the Bible says God created it."

That would be circular.

Instead investigate whether the universe has characteristics associated with contingency:

- it changes;
- it contains dependent entities;
- its physical states could apparently have been different;
- it has a particular structure;
- modern cosmology indicates a finite hot early state for our observable universe;
- physical constants appear contingent rather than logically necessary.

None of these individually proves that the universe is contingent in the strict metaphysical sense.

But together they make the question of an external necessary ground philosophically serious.

---

# 7. Kalam Cosmological Argument

A different argument focuses on the beginning of the universe.

### Argument

1. Whatever begins to exist has a cause.
2. The universe began to exist.
3. Therefore, the universe has a cause.

This is commonly called the **Kalam Cosmological Argument**.

---

# 8. Premise 1: Whatever Begins to Exist Has a Cause

The intuition is straightforward:

Things do not normally appear from absolute nothing.

If a house exists, we ask what produced it.

If a computer exists, we ask how it came to exist.

If a physical event begins, we investigate its causal conditions.

The argument therefore asks:

> Why should the universe be an exception?

---

# 9. Premise 2: Did the Universe Begin?

This is a major philosophical and scientific question.

Relevant considerations include:

- the expansion of the universe;
- the finite age of the observable universe;
- thermodynamic considerations;
- cosmological models;
- philosophical arguments against an actual infinite regress of past events.

The standard Big Bang model describes the early hot, dense state of our observable universe.

However:

> **The Big Bang is not simply identical to "creation from nothing."**

A scientifically careful argument must not claim that cosmology has directly proven Genesis or creatio ex nihilo.

Cosmology informs the philosophical argument; it does not replace it.

---

# 10. What Caused the Universe?

If the universe began, its cause cannot simply be:

> another ordinary physical object inside the universe.

The cause would have to transcend the physical universe as we know it.

This points toward something:

- non-physical;
- enormously powerful;
- independent of the universe;
- not itself dependent upon physical matter and space-time.

This does not yet establish Christianity.

It establishes a philosophical problem for a purely physical account of ultimate reality.

---

# 11. The Problem of an Infinite Regress

Suppose every contingent thing requires another contingent explanation.

We have:

> A depends on B  
> B depends on C  
> C depends on D  
> D depends on E  
> ...

If the entire series consists only of contingent explanations, the question remains:

> **Why does the entire series exist?**

Adding more contingent explanations does not automatically provide an ultimate explanation.

An infinite number of dependent things would still be dependent.

Therefore the argument seeks:

> **a non-contingent foundation.**

---

# 12. Important Qualification: Not Every Infinite Regress Is Automatically Impossible

Do not argue:

> "Infinite regress is always impossible."

That is too broad.

Some philosophical regressions may be harmless.

The stronger argument is about an **explanatory regress**:

> If every member depends upon something else and nothing possesses existence independently, what ultimately grounds the existence of the entire dependent order?

This is stronger than merely saying:

> "You can't count to infinity."

---

# 13. Argument from Change and Causation

Aristotle and Thomas Aquinas developed arguments from change and causation.

The basic intuition is:

> Things that change move from potentiality to actuality.

For example:

- wood can become hot;
- a seed can become a tree;
- a person can learn;
- matter can change configuration.

Something actual must account for the actualization of a potential.

This leads to a deeper question:

> **What ultimately grounds the causal order of changing things?**

Aquinas argues toward a first cause in the relevant explanatory sense.

---

# 14. Aquinas: The Five Ways

Thomas Aquinas presented five approaches:

1. From motion/change.
2. From efficient causation.
3. From contingency and necessity.
4. From gradation.
5. From governance/order.

These are often misunderstood as five versions of:

> "Everything has a cause."

They are not.

Aquinas' arguments are more metaphysical and concern the dependence of things here and now.

His arguments should therefore be studied in their original philosophical context rather than reduced to internet summaries.

---

# 15. Argument from Intelligibility

The universe is remarkably intelligible.

Human beings can:

- formulate mathematical descriptions;
- discover physical laws;
- predict phenomena;
- reason from abstract principles;
- understand structures that existed before humanity.

The question is:

> **Why is reality intelligible to rational minds?**

A theistic explanation is:

> Reality ultimately comes from an ordering intelligence.

This does not prove God by itself.

But it contributes to the cumulative case.

---

# 16. Fine-Tuning

Modern cosmology and physics raise questions concerning the apparent sensitivity of physical parameters relevant to a life-permitting universe.

The basic observation is:

> Certain physical constants and initial conditions fall within ranges compatible with complex structures and life.

Three broad explanatory options are commonly discussed:

### 1. Necessity

The constants could not have been otherwise.

### 2. Chance

The universe happens to have life-permitting conditions.

### 3. Design

The conditions are the result of intelligent selection.

The fine-tuning argument argues that design deserves serious consideration.

---

# 17. Do Not Overstate Fine-Tuning

Avoid saying:

> "Science has proven God because the universe is fine-tuned."

That is not established.

There are serious alternatives:

- unknown physical necessity;
- multiverse hypotheses;
- selection effects;
- unknown cosmological mechanisms.

The proper apologetic claim is:

> **Fine-tuning provides evidence that can rationally support theism, particularly when combined with other arguments.**

---

# 18. The Multiverse Objection

A skeptic may say:

> "A multiverse explains fine-tuning."

The response:

A multiverse may provide a possible naturalistic explanation.

But it does not necessarily eliminate the philosophical questions:

- Why does the multiverse exist?
- Why does it have the laws that generate universes?
- Why does the underlying mathematical/physical structure exist?
- Is the multiverse itself contingent?
- What explains the meta-laws?

Therefore:

> **A multiverse can potentially address the fine-tuning question without necessarily answering the ultimate-explanation question.**

---

# 19. Argument from Objective Morality

Consider claims such as:

> Torturing an innocent child purely for pleasure is objectively wrong.

Not merely:

> "I dislike it."

But:

> **It is wrong regardless of whether someone approves of it.**

If objective moral truths exist, we need an explanation for their objective and binding character.

The theistic argument proposes:

> Objective moral obligations are grounded in a transcendent moral reality.

Ultimately:

> **God is the foundation of objective moral value and obligation.**

---

# 20. The Euthyphro Dilemma

A classic objection asks:

> "Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?"

If goodness exists independently of God, God appears unnecessary.

If goodness is merely whatever God commands, morality may seem arbitrary.

Classical Christian theology gives a third possibility:

> **Goodness is grounded in God's nature.**

God does not arbitrarily invent goodness.

Rather:

> God is the fullness of being and goodness.

Therefore God's commands express His good nature rather than creating morality by arbitrary decision.

---

# 21. Argument from Consciousness

Physical objects can be described in terms of:

- mass;
- charge;
- location;
- energy;
- physical structure.

But consciousness includes:

- subjective experience;
- awareness;
- intentionality;
- first-person perspective;
- rational thought.

The question is:

> **Why does subjective consciousness exist at all?**

Materialism attempts various explanations.

The theistic view proposes:

> Consciousness ultimately exists because reality is grounded in a fundamental intelligence.

Again, this is a cumulative argument, not a standalone mathematical proof.

---

# 22. Argument from Reason

There is another interesting philosophical issue.

If human reasoning is entirely the product of non-rational evolutionary processes, why trust our reasoning faculties to discover abstract truths?

Evolution selects for survival.

It does not directly select for:

> metaphysical truth.

A theistic account proposes:

> Rational minds are ultimately grounded in a rational source of reality.

This gives a natural connection between:

> rational universe → rational creatures → rational knowledge.

This argument requires careful formulation because evolutionary naturalism can provide substantial responses.

---

# 23. Argument from Information and Order

Biological systems contain extraordinary amounts of functional information.

The question is:

> **What ultimately explains the existence of information-bearing systems?**

Do not argue:

> "DNA is complex, therefore God."

Complexity alone does not establish design.

Instead distinguish:

- complexity;
- specified/functionally organized information;
- physical mechanisms;
- origin of life;
- origin of biological information.

The origin-of-life question remains an active scientific field.

A philosophical argument from biological information can support design arguments, but should not be treated as settled science.

---

# 24. The Argument from Universals

Human beings reason about abstract realities such as:

- numbers;
- logical principles;
- mathematical relationships;
- geometric truths;
- propositions.

For example:

> 2 + 2 = 4

is not a physical object.

The question is:

> **What is the ontological status of abstract truths?**

Different philosophical systems give different answers.

Theism can interpret mathematical and logical order as grounded in a divine intellect.

This is not a proof by itself, but it contributes to the broader case.

---

# 25. The Argument from Beauty

Human beings recognize:

- beauty;
- harmony;
- proportion;
- music;
- artistic order.

One can ask:

> Why is beauty capable of moving rational creatures so profoundly?

Aesthetic arguments are weaker than contingency or cosmological arguments because subjective preferences vary.

Nevertheless, objective aesthetic value may fit naturally within a theistic worldview.

---

# 26. Cumulative Case

No single argument needs to carry the entire burden.

Consider the combined evidence:

| Argument | Question | Theistic Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Contingency | Why does contingent reality exist? | Necessary ground |
| Cosmological | Why does the universe exist/begin? | Transcendent cause |
| Change/causation | What grounds dependent causal activity? | First/primary cause |
| Fine-tuning | Why life-permitting conditions? | Possible intelligent design |
| Intelligibility | Why is reality mathematically intelligible? | Rational source |
| Morality | Why objective moral obligations? | Moral foundation |
| Consciousness | Why subjective awareness? | Mind as fundamental |
| Reason | Why can minds know truth? | Rational ground |
| Order/information | Why organized complexity? | Possible intelligence |
| Universals | What grounds abstract truths? | Divine intellect |

The goal is not:

> "One argument proves everything."

The goal is:

> **A coherent worldview explains multiple independent features of reality.**

---

# 27. The Best-Explanation Principle

A philosophical argument often asks:

> **Which worldview explains the evidence most adequately?**

Compare:

### Naturalism

Ultimate reality is fundamentally physical/natural.

### Theism

Ultimate reality includes a necessary transcendent intelligence.

The comparison should examine:

- existence;
- causation;
- order;
- intelligibility;
- consciousness;
- morality;
- reason;
- fine-tuning;
- human rationality.

The question becomes:

> **Which worldview provides the more comprehensive explanation with fewer unexplained ultimate facts?**

---

# 28. The "Who Created the Universe?" Reversal

The skeptic may ask:

> "If God created the universe, why doesn't God need a creator?"

Respond:

> **If God needed a creator, God would not be the ultimate explanation.**

Then ask:

> "What is your candidate for ultimate reality?"

Possible answers include:

- universe;
- multiverse;
- laws of physics;
- quantum field;
- brute fact;
- necessary mathematical reality.

Then ask:

> **Why is that ultimate reality necessary rather than contingent?**

This shifts the discussion from:

> "Who created God?"

to:

> **"What ultimately explains reality?"**

---

# 29. "Why Can't the Universe Be a Brute Fact?"

A brute fact is:

> Something that simply exists without further explanation.

This is logically possible.

But it has a cost.

If the skeptic says:

> "The universe just exists."

the Christian can consistently answer:

> "That is possible as a philosophical stopping point, but why should we prefer an unexplained contingent universe over a necessary explanation?"

The debate becomes one of explanatory adequacy.

---

# 30. "Who Created the Necessary Being?"

If God is necessary, the question:

> "Who created God?"

misunderstands the claim.

A necessary being is not something that:

> happens to exist.

Rather:

> **Its non-existence is impossible.**

If something can be created or destroyed, it is not necessary in the relevant sense.

---

# 31. Does "Necessary" Mean Logically Necessary?

Be precise.

There are different forms of necessity.

### Logical necessity

Something whose denial involves contradiction.

### Metaphysical necessity

Something that cannot fail to exist given the fundamental nature of reality.

### Physical necessity

Something required by the laws of a particular physical system.

Classical theism generally argues for:

> **metaphysical necessity.**

Do not casually equate this with:

> "We can prove God's existence like we prove a mathematical theorem."

---

# 32. Can Science Disprove God?

Science investigates the physical world using empirical methods.

God, in classical theism, is not proposed as:

> a physical object somewhere in space.

Therefore:

> **The absence of a measurable physical God does not constitute evidence that God does not exist.**

The question of God's existence is partly:

- metaphysical;
- philosophical;
- theological.

Science can provide evidence relevant to the discussion, but it cannot by itself exhaust the question.

---

# 33. "God Is a God of the Gaps"

This is an important objection.

Bad argument:

> "Science doesn't know how X happened, therefore God did it."

That is a **God-of-the-gaps** argument.

The classical arguments are different.

They ask:

> **What ultimately explains existence itself?**

or:

> **What grounds contingent reality?**

or:

> **Why is reality intelligible?**

These are not merely gaps in current scientific knowledge.

They are questions about the philosophical foundations of reality.

---

# 34. "Quantum Mechanics Allows Something From Nothing"

This objection often uses "nothing" differently from philosophy.

In physics, "nothing" may refer to:

- a quantum vacuum;
- a field;
- a physical state;
- a mathematical framework;
- laws governing the system.

Philosophical nothing means:

> **No things whatsoever.**

No:

- space;
- time;
- matter;
- energy;
- fields;
- laws;
- physical states.

Therefore:

> **A quantum vacuum is not philosophical nothing.**

If a physical model produces a universe from a quantum state, we can still ask:

> Why does the quantum framework exist?

---

# 35. "God Is Just Another Explanation"

A skeptic may say:

> "You are simply adding another entity."

The classical theistic response is:

> God is not proposed as another object inside the universe.

God is proposed as:

> **the ground of existence of the universe and contingent reality.**

This is analogous to distinguishing:

> a physical object

from:

> the metaphysical ground that explains why physical objects exist.

---

# 36. Personal or Impersonal Ultimate Reality?

Suppose we establish:

> A necessary foundation exists.

Does it have to be personal?

This requires additional reasoning.

If the cause is:

- eternal;
- timeless;
- non-physical;
- enormously powerful;

then one can ask why a temporal universe begins rather than existing eternally.

Some philosophical arguments suggest that a personal agent provides an explanation because an agent can freely initiate an effect without being temporally prior in the ordinary physical sense.

This is a sophisticated step and should not be presented as automatic.

---

# 37. From Necessary Being to Divine Attributes

Once a necessary transcendent ground is established, ask what properties it must possess.

### Necessary

It cannot depend upon another contingent thing.

### Transcendent

It is not merely another physical component of the universe.

### Immaterial

If it grounds space-time and matter, it cannot simply be another material object within space-time.

### Eternal

If it is the ultimate ground of time and contingent reality, it is not dependent upon temporal succession in the ordinary sense.

### Powerful

It is sufficient to ground all contingent reality.

### Intelligent

Further arguments from order, rationality, information, consciousness, and intentionality can support intelligence.

### Good

The moral argument can support the grounding of objective goodness.

These conclusions should be argued individually.

---

# 38. What This Phase Does NOT Yet Prove

Do not claim that this phase alone proves:

- Christianity;
- the Trinity;
- the Incarnation;
- the Resurrection;
- the Bible;
- Catholicism;
- the Catholic Church.

Those require separate arguments.

The conclusion here is:

> **Theism is rationally justified, and a necessary transcendent foundation of reality is philosophically defensible.**

---

# 39. Why Establish God Before the Resurrection?

This is strategically important.

Suppose we establish:

### Historical evidence

Jesus died.

Jesus' followers claimed appearances.

The Resurrection proclamation was extremely early.

Paul converted.

James converted.

The empty tomb is historically defensible.

### Philosophical evidence

God exists.

Then the hypothesis:

> **God raised Jesus from the dead**

is no longer automatically dismissed as impossible.

The historical and philosophical arguments reinforce each other.

---

# 40. But Do Not Use God as a Shortcut

Avoid:

> "God exists, therefore Jesus rose."

That does not follow.

There are many theistic religions.

Instead:

> God exists  
> +  
> Jesus died  
> +  
> Jesus' earliest followers claimed appearances  
> +  
> early Resurrection proclamation  
> +  
> Paul and James  
> +  
> empty tomb, if established  
> +  
> alternative explanations  
> ↓  
> **Resurrection becomes a historical hypothesis worth evaluating.**

---

# 42. Strongest Arguments to Prioritize

For a limited-time apologetics presentation, prioritize:

## Tier 1

### 1. Contingency

> Why does anything contingent exist at all?

### 2. Cosmological argument

> Why does the universe exist, and does it have an ultimate cause?

### 3. Argument from intelligibility

> Why is reality rationally and mathematically intelligible?

## Tier 2

### 4. Fine-tuning

> Why does the universe have life-permitting parameters?

### 5. Morality

> Why are some things objectively right or wrong?

### 6. Consciousness

> Why does subjective consciousness exist?

The strongest case is cumulative.

---

# 43. What a Skeptic Can Reasonably Accept

A serious skeptic may agree:

- the universe exists;
- contingent things exist;
- cosmology raises questions about ultimate origins;
- consciousness is difficult to reduce completely;
- objective morality is philosophically debated;
- fine-tuning has legitimate philosophical implications;
- the universe is mathematically intelligible.

The skeptic can nevertheless reject theism.

That is acceptable.

The objective is not to pretend there is no debate.

The objective is to show:

> **Atheism is not the default conclusion forced upon us by science.**

Theism is a serious philosophical position.

---

# 44. What We Should Not Claim

Avoid these weak arguments:

### Bad argument

> "Scientists cannot explain X, therefore God."

### Bad argument

> "The Big Bang proves Genesis."

### Bad argument

> "Evolution is false, therefore God."

### Bad argument

> "DNA is complicated, therefore God."

### Bad argument

> "The universe is beautiful, therefore Christianity."

### Bad argument

> "Quantum physics proves God."

### Bad argument

> "Science cannot disprove God, therefore God exists."

These arguments overreach.

A stronger apologetic acknowledges the limits of each individual argument.

---

# 45. The Cumulative Philosophical Conclusion

The case can be summarized:

> Reality contains contingent things.
>
> Contingent reality does not appear to contain within itself an ultimate explanation for its existence.
>
> An ultimate explanation must not itself merely be another contingent object.
>
> This points toward a necessary foundation of reality.
>
> Cosmological reasoning points toward a transcendent cause.
>
> Fine-tuning provides evidence compatible with intelligent ordering.
>
> The intelligibility of reality is compatible with a rational source.
>
> Consciousness and reason raise further questions for a purely material account.
>
> Objective moral values, if they exist, point toward a transcendent foundation of morality.
>
> Taken cumulatively, these considerations provide substantial philosophical support for the existence of God.

---

# 46. Transition to Phase 4: Resurrection

The conclusion of this phase should deliberately lead into the next phase.

> **If God exists, then a miracle is not metaphysically impossible.**

But that does **not** mean every miracle claim is true.

Therefore the next question is:

> **Did God actually raise Jesus from the dead?**

Now return to history.

We investigate:

1. Jesus existed.
2. Jesus was crucified.
3. Jesus died.
4. Jesus was buried.
5. Resurrection belief appeared extremely early.
6. The disciples claimed appearances.
7. Paul converted.
8. James converted.
9. The empty tomb is historically defensible.
10. Competing explanations are evaluated.
11. Resurrection is compared as the best explanation.

The two lines of argument then converge:

```text
PHILOSOPHY
God exists
      ↓
Miracles are possible

              +

HISTORY
Jesus died
      ↓
Early Resurrection belief
      ↓
Appearances
      ↓
Paul / James
      ↓
Empty tomb
      ↓
Competing explanations
      ↓
Resurrection
```

This gives the apologetic a non-circular structure.

---

# 47. Core Conclusion of Phase 3

The target conclusion should be stated carefully:

> **The existence of God is not a conclusion that science forces upon us, nor is it a mere assumption of religious faith. Classical and contemporary philosophical arguments provide rational grounds for believing that contingent reality has a necessary foundation, that the universe has an ultimate explanation, and that features such as intelligibility, consciousness, morality, and fine-tuning are compatible with—and cumulatively supportive of—a transcendent intelligent source of reality.**

The next historical question is:

> **If such a God exists, did He raise Jesus from the dead?**

# 49. Final Apologetics Chain

This phase belongs within the larger project:

```text
PHASE 1
Jesus existed
        ↓
PHASE 2
The Bible / New Testament is a legitimate
and substantially reliable historical source
        ↓
PHASE 3
God exists
        ↓
PHASE 4
Jesus rose from the dead
        ↓
PHASE 5
Jesus is God
        ↓
PHASE 6
Jesus founded a Church
        ↓
PHASE 7
The apostolic Church continued historically
        ↓
PHASE 8
Scripture + Apostolic Tradition + Apostolic Succession
        ↓
PHASE 9
Catholic Church
```

The key methodological rule is:

> **Establish each conclusion before using it as a premise for the next conclusion.**

That prevents circular apologetics and makes the entire case much more difficult to dismiss.
