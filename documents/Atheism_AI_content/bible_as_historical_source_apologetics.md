# Using the Bible as a Historical Source

## A Non-Circular Historical Case for the New Testament

### Purpose

This document develops an apologetic argument for treating the Bible—especially the New Testament—not merely as a theological text, but also as a collection of ancient historical sources.

The goal is **not** to begin with:

> "The Bible is inspired by God, therefore it is historically true."

That would be circular when speaking with an atheist or skeptical historian.

Instead, the argument begins with ordinary historical questions:

- When were the documents written?
- Who wrote them?
- How close were the authors to the events?
- Did the authors have access to eyewitnesses?
- Are there independent traditions?
- Do external sources confirm the setting?
- Does archaeology support the geographical, political, and cultural background?
- How were the manuscripts transmitted?
- Can textual criticism recover the earliest attainable form of the text?
- Are there reasons to regard the authors as deliberate fabricators?

Only after those questions are addressed should theological claims be evaluated.

---

# 1. What Does It Mean to Call the Bible a "Historical Source"?

A historical source does **not** have to be:

- secular;
- unbiased;
- inspired;
- infallible;
- written by an atheist;
- free from theological convictions.

Ancient historians routinely use sources written by people with strong political, religious, or cultural commitments.

The proper question is:

> **Does the source contain recoverable historical information, and can its claims be tested against other evidence?**

The Bible can therefore be studied in at least two distinct ways:

### Theological reading

> What does Scripture teach?

### Historical reading

> What information about the ancient world and early Christianity can be responsibly recovered from these documents?

The second question can be investigated without first accepting Christianity.

---

# 2. The Bible Is a Library, Not a Single Historical Document

The term "Bible" can hide an important fact.

The Bible is a collection of documents written:

- by different authors;
- in different periods;
- in different genres;
- for different audiences;
- under different historical circumstances.

For the New Testament, the relevant sources include:

- Paul's letters;
- the four canonical Gospels;
- Acts;
- other epistles;
- Revelation.

These documents should not be treated as if they were written by one author on one occasion.

That allows historians to compare:

> one source against another,

rather than merely saying:

> "The Bible says it."

---

# 3. The New Testament Should Be Compared With Other Ancient Sources

A common skeptical objection is:

> "The Bible is religious propaganda."

The correct response is not:

> "No, it isn't."

The better response is:

> **"Even if a source has an agenda, why would that automatically make its historical information worthless?"**

Josephus had political and apologetic interests.

Tacitus had Roman senatorial biases.

Suetonius wrote with his own literary purposes.

Ancient historians were not modern detached journalists.

Historians therefore evaluate sources using multiple criteria rather than demanding complete neutrality.

---

# 4. Criterion: Chronological Proximity

One of the most important questions is:

> **How close is the source to the events it describes?**

For Jesus, the undisputed Pauline letters are extremely important.

Jesus' death is commonly dated around AD 30.

Paul's undisputed letters are generally dated to the AD 50s.

That places some written Christian evidence roughly:

> **20 years after Jesus' death.**

For ancient history, this is unusually close.

The four Gospels are later than Paul's earliest letters, but still belong to the first century.

This does not prove every statement in them.

It does mean they cannot reasonably be dismissed merely as late medieval legends.

---

# 5. Criterion: Eyewitness Access

Paul is particularly important because he identifies people from the first generation of Christianity.

## Galatians 1:18-19

Paul reports meeting:

- Cephas/Peter;
- James, the brother of Jesus.

This creates an important historical connection:

> Jesus -> Peter and James -> Paul -> Paul's letters

Paul was therefore not receiving Christian traditions from people who lived centuries after Jesus.

---

# 6. Criterion: Earlier Traditions Preserved in Later Documents

A document can preserve traditions older than the document itself.

The clearest example is:

## 1 Corinthians 15:3-8

Paul writes:

> "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received..."

He then gives the core tradition:

> Christ died  
> Christ was buried  
> Christ was raised  
> Christ appeared to Cephas  
> Christ appeared to the Twelve  
> Christ appeared to James  
> Christ appeared to Paul

Paul's language indicates that he received this tradition from an earlier source.

This means:

> **The date of the letter is not necessarily the date when the tradition originated.**

The Resurrection proclamation therefore reaches back to the earliest period of Christianity.

Many scholars date the formation of this tradition very early, although the exact date is debated.

---

# 7. Criterion: Multiple Attestation

Historians give greater attention to claims that appear in multiple independent or partially independent traditions.

For Jesus, relevant traditions include:

- Paul's letters;
- Mark;
- Matthew;
- Luke;
- John;
- Acts;
- early Christian tradition reflected in later writers.

Not every Gospel account is completely independent of every other account.

Therefore avoid saying:

> "We have four completely independent eyewitness biographies."

That is too simplistic.

A more defensible claim is:

> **The New Testament contains multiple literary traditions and streams of testimony that can be compared critically.**

---

# 8. Criterion: External Corroboration

The New Testament should be compared with non-Christian sources.

## Josephus

Josephus provides evidence concerning:

- Jesus;
- James, the brother of Jesus;
- first-century Jewish society;
- the political and religious environment.

The passage concerning James in *Antiquities* 20 is especially useful.

The longer reference to Jesus in *Antiquities* 18 has textual complications and should be handled cautiously.

## Tacitus

Tacitus reports that:

- Christians took their name from Christus;
- Christus was executed under Pontius Pilate;
- the execution occurred during Tiberius' reign;
- Christianity subsequently spread.

Tacitus is particularly valuable because he was not a Christian source.

## Pliny the Younger

Pliny describes Christians worshipping Christ and gathering for worship.

He demonstrates that the Christian movement was well established in the early second century.

### Important principle

External sources do not need to repeat every Gospel event to corroborate the overall historical setting.

A source can independently confirm:

- the existence of Jesus;
- the existence of Christianity;
- Jesus' execution;
- the existence of Christian worship;
- important Christian leaders.

---

# 9. Criterion: Archaeological Corroboration

Archaeology generally cannot prove a theological proposition such as:

> "Jesus is the Son of God."

But it can test whether the biblical historical environment is real.

Examples include:

## Pontius Pilate

The Pilate Stone from Caesarea identifies:

> Pontius Pilate

and connects him with the administration of Judea under Tiberius.

This independently confirms a major person in the Gospel narrative.

## Nazareth

Archaeology supports the existence of a first-century settlement at Nazareth.

This matters because Nazareth is part of the geographical setting of Jesus' life.

## Capernaum

Archaeological remains support the existence and importance of Capernaum as a first-century settlement.

## First-century Jerusalem

Archaeological evidence confirms:

- Jewish settlement;
- Roman administration;
- burial practices;
- architecture;
- social and economic conditions.

## Roman Crucifixion

The remains associated with Yehohanan provide archaeological evidence that Roman crucifixion was practiced in first-century Judea.

The correct conclusion is:

> **Archaeology corroborates the historical world described by the New Testament.**

It does not independently prove every theological claim.

---

# 10. Criterion: Cultural and Historical Fit

A historical source should fit its alleged historical environment.

The Gospels contain numerous details relating to:

- Jewish purification;
- Temple worship;
- Pharisees;
- Sadducees;
- Roman authority;
- Galilean geography;
- Jewish burial;
- Passover;
- first-century Jewish expectations;
- Hebrew/Aramaic expressions.

These details are relevant because a source that consistently misunderstands its claimed historical environment would be less credible.

This criterion is not proof.

It is cumulative evidence.

---

# 11. Criterion: Embarrassing or Difficult Details

Ancient authors often had reasons to present their movements favorably.

Yet the Gospel traditions contain details that could be inconvenient for the early Christian movement.

Examples include:

- Peter's denial;
- the disciples' repeated misunderstandings;
- the disciples' abandonment of Jesus;
- women being presented as the first discoverers of the empty tomb;
- Jesus receiving baptism from John;
- the shameful crucifixion;
- the disciples being portrayed as frightened after Jesus' death.

These details do not automatically prove historical truth.

But they are relevant to the question:

> **Does the source look like a simple piece of idealized propaganda?**

A more careful conclusion is:

> Some Gospel traditions contain features that are difficult to explain purely as deliberate image-management.

---

# 12. Criterion: Enemy Attestation

Sometimes an opponent confirms that a movement or claim existed.

This is valuable because the opponent has no obvious reason to help the movement.

Examples:

## Tacitus

Confirms:

- Christus;
- execution;
- Christianity;
- Christian persecution.

## The hostile tradition concerning the disciples' alleged theft of Jesus' body

Matthew records an accusation that the disciples stole the body.

Even if one does not accept Matthew's complete account, the existence of an early polemical explanation is historically interesting.

The important principle is:

> **A hostile explanation may inadvertently confirm that an underlying event or claim was being disputed.**

But this must be used cautiously. It does not automatically establish the truth of the Christian claim.

---

# 13. Criterion: Manuscript Transmission

Historical reliability has another layer:

> **What did the original documents actually say?**

This is different from:

> **Are the things they said true?**

Textual criticism addresses the first question.

The New Testament manuscript tradition includes:

- Greek manuscripts;
- papyri;
- ancient translations;
- quotations from early Christian writers;
- major codices.

Important witnesses include:

- Codex Sinaiticus;
- Codex Vaticanus;
- early papyri such as P52, P46, P66, and P75.

There are textual variants.

That should be openly acknowledged.

However:

> **The existence of textual variants does not mean the original text is unknowable.**

Manuscript comparison allows textual critics to identify many copying differences and reconstruct the earliest attainable text with substantial confidence in most passages.

---

# 14. Textual Reliability Is Not the Same as Historical Reliability

This distinction is essential.

Suppose we establish that:

> Paul really wrote 1 Corinthians.

That does not prove:

> everything Paul says is true.

Likewise:

> We can reconstruct what the Gospel authors wrote.

does not automatically prove:

> every Gospel event happened exactly as narrated.

The argument has layers:

### Layer 1

Can we recover the text?

### Layer 2

Is the text an early and historically relevant source?

### Layer 3

Is the author's testimony credible?

### Layer 4

Does external evidence corroborate the claims?

### Layer 5

What explanation best accounts for the evidence?

This prevents apologetics from making invalid jumps.

---

# 15. Genre Matters

Not every biblical book is a modern historical biography.

The Bible contains:

- narrative;
- poetry;
- wisdom literature;
- prophecy;
- law;
- letters;
- apocalyptic literature;
- Gospel literature.

Therefore:

> **Historical interpretation must account for genre.**

A Psalm should not be read like a police report.

Revelation should not be read like a modern newspaper.

Likewise, the Gospels should be evaluated according to what ancient biography and historiography were actually like.

---

# 16. Luke's Historical Method

Luke provides a particularly explicit statement about his method.

## Luke 1:1-4

Luke says that:

- many accounts had been undertaken;
- traditions had been handed down by eyewitnesses and ministers of the word;
- he had investigated matters carefully;
- he intended to write an orderly account.

This does not prove Luke's conclusions.

But it is significant evidence about how the author presents his historical method.

Luke should therefore be evaluated as an ancient historical author, not simply dismissed as theological fiction.

---

# 17. Acts as a Historical Source

Acts contains a large amount of geographical and political detail.

Examples include:

- Roman administrative titles;
- cities;
- governors;
- travel routes;
- local political structures;
- Jewish institutions.

Some details have been corroborated by archaeology and inscriptions.

A famous example is Luke's use of political terminology.

Historians have observed that Acts' terminology concerning Roman and local officials often fits the first-century environment.

Again:

> **Accuracy in geographical or political details does not prove every supernatural event.**

But it establishes historical competence in areas that can be independently checked.

---

# 18. Can a Religious Source Be Historically Reliable?

Yes.

This is an important philosophical point.

A person's motivation does not logically determine whether their testimony is true.

Suppose a Roman historian strongly supports the Roman Empire.

We do not conclude:

> "Therefore nothing he says is historical."

We ask:

- What did he know?
- What sources did he use?
- Was he close to the events?
- Does independent evidence support him?
- Does he make demonstrable errors?
- What was his purpose?

The same standards should be applied to Christian sources.

---

# 19. Comparing the Bible With Other Ancient Historians

This is where a useful apologetic comparison can be made.

We accept historical information about ancient people despite:

- limited surviving manuscripts;
- authors with biases;
- late copies;
- conflicting accounts;
- incomplete archaeology.

Examples include:

- Alexander the Great;
- Julius Caesar;
- Augustus;
- Tiberius;
- Pontius Pilate;
- Herod the Great;
- Lapu-Lapu;
- various Egyptian pharaohs.

We do not demand:

> "Give me a video recorded during their lifetime."

Instead we assess:

> **How strong is the total evidence?**

The same standard should be applied to Jesus.

---

# 20. Jesus Compared With Other Ancient Figures

Be careful with the statement:

> "There is more evidence for Jesus than for every other ancient person."

That is too broad.

Some ancient figures have extremely abundant surviving evidence.

A stronger and more defensible argument is:

> **For a first-century Jewish teacher from a relatively obscure provincial region, the quantity and variety of evidence concerning Jesus are remarkably substantial.**

Jesus has evidence from:

- early Christian letters;
- multiple Gospel traditions;
- early Christian communities;
- non-Christian writers;
- archaeology;
- hostile testimony;
- later historical references.

And importantly:

> **Some of the earliest evidence is extremely close chronologically to Jesus.**

---

# 21. A Useful Comparison: Lapu-Lapu

Lapu-Lapu is an interesting Filipino comparison.

Lapu-Lapu lived in the early sixteenth century.

Jesus lived in the first century.

The chronological gap between us and Lapu-Lapu is therefore roughly five centuries.

The chronological gap between us and Jesus is roughly twenty centuries.

Yet historians confidently affirm that Lapu-Lapu existed.

Why?

Because historical evidence does not require modern photographs or recordings.

We rely on:

- written accounts;
- archaeological evidence;
- oral traditions;
- material remains;
- contemporary or near-contemporary sources;
- later corroboration.

Therefore:

> **The fact that Jesus lived two thousand years ago does not make historical investigation impossible.**

The proper question is:

> **How much evidence survives, how early is it, how independent is it, and how well does it survive critical examination?**

Do not claim that the historical evidence for Jesus is necessarily "more abundant than Lapu-Lapu" without defining what "abundant" means.

Instead compare categories of evidence.

---

# 22. A Useful Comparison: Roman Emperors

The same principle applies to Roman emperors.

We accept that:

- Augustus existed;
- Tiberius existed;
- Claudius existed;
- Nero existed.

We use:

- coins;
- inscriptions;
- historians;
- monuments;
- administrative records;
- archaeological remains.

Jesus does not have the same type of evidence because he was not an emperor.

Therefore the fair comparison is not:

> "Why doesn't Jesus have imperial coins?"

It is:

> **"Given Jesus' social status, geographical setting, and historical period, is the evidence proportionate and credible?"**

For a first-century Jewish teacher, the surviving literary evidence is remarkable.

---

# 23. Why Josephus and Tacitus Matter

They are important for a particular reason:

They show that Jesus and the Christian movement do not exist only inside Christian literature.

### Christian evidence

Paul + Gospels + Acts

### Non-Christian evidence

Josephus + Tacitus + Pliny + other later references

### Archaeological evidence

Pilate + Nazareth + first-century Judea + crucifixion context

This creates a convergence:

> **Multiple categories of evidence point toward the same historical environment.**

---

# 24. What an Atheist Can Reasonably Say

A serious atheist can accept:

- Jesus existed;
- Jesus was crucified;
- Paul existed;
- early Christians believed Jesus appeared to them;
- Christianity began in the first century.

and still reject Christianity.

That is intellectually possible.

The disagreement then moves to:

> **What best explains those facts?**

The atheist may argue:

> visions + legend + psychological experiences + unknown natural causes.

The Christian argues:

> **Resurrection.**

The debate therefore becomes explanatory rather than merely textual.

---

# 25. What Historical Evidence Can Establish

Historical evidence can establish things such as:

> Jesus existed.

> Jesus was crucified.

> Christians believed he rose.

> Paul converted.

> James became a Christian leader.

> Christian communities existed very early.

> Certain biblical locations, people, customs, and political circumstances are historically grounded.

Historical reasoning can also argue for:

> **the Resurrection as the best explanation of the total evidence.**

But historical evidence should not be presented as mathematical certainty.

---

# 26. What Historical Evidence Cannot Establish By Itself

History alone does not automatically establish:

> "The Catholic Church is true."

Nor does archaeology automatically establish:

> "Jesus is God."

Nor does manuscript quantity establish:

> "The Resurrection happened."

Those require additional arguments.

A rigorous apologetic therefore proceeds step by step.

---

# 27. The Non-Circular Argument

The complete structure can be expressed:

```text
1. The New Testament is an ancient collection of documents.
             ↓
2. These documents can be dated and critically analyzed.
             ↓
3. Some are very early and connected to eyewitnesses.
             ↓
4. Their historical claims can be compared with external evidence.
             ↓
5. External evidence corroborates significant people,
   places, customs, and events.
             ↓
6. Therefore the New Testament is a legitimate
   historical source.
             ↓
7. The historical data concerning Jesus can be extracted
   and critically evaluated.
             ↓
8. The Resurrection hypothesis explains the total
   evidence better than competing hypotheses.
             ↓
9. If Jesus rose, his claims deserve extraordinary authority.
             ↓
10. Investigate what Jesus taught about God, revelation,
    Scripture, and the Church.
```

---

# 28. The Key Apologetic Principle

Do not argue:

> **"The Bible is true because the Bible says it is true."**

Argue:

> **"The Bible contains ancient historical documents. Those documents can be examined using the same historical methods applied to other ancient sources. When critically evaluated, they provide significant early evidence concerning Jesus and the origins of Christianity. Their theological claims can then be evaluated on the basis of the historical events they report."**

This is a much stronger starting point.

# 31. Final Argument

The proper claim is not:

> **"The Bible is historically true because it is the Bible."**

The stronger argument is:

> **The New Testament consists of ancient documents that can be investigated independently of their theological claims. Some of its earliest documents were produced within the first generation of Christianity and are connected to people who knew the original disciples. The documents contain geographical, political, cultural, and historical information that can be tested against external evidence. Non-Christian writers such as Josephus and Tacitus independently confirm important elements of the historical framework. Archaeology confirms numerous aspects of the first-century Judean environment. Manuscript evidence permits substantial reconstruction of the New Testament text despite known textual variants. Therefore the New Testament is a legitimate and historically significant source for investigating Jesus and early Christianity.**

The next question is not:

> "Do we believe the Bible?"

It is:

> **"What does the historical evidence contained in these sources tell us actually happened to Jesus?"**

That takes us directly to the Resurrection.

---

# 32. Apologetics Sequence

The recommended sequence is:

```text
PHILOSOPHY
    ↓
Does God exist?
    ↓
HISTORY
    ↓
Did Jesus exist?
    ↓
Are the Christian sources legitimate historical sources?
    ↓
What happened to Jesus?
    ↓
Did Jesus rise from the dead?
    ↓
PHILOSOPHY + HISTORY
    ↓
If Jesus rose, what does this imply?
    ↓
Who did Jesus claim to be?
    ↓
Did Jesus establish a Church?
    ↓
What happened to that Church historically?
    ↓
Apostolic succession
    ↓
Scripture + Tradition
    ↓
Catholic Church
```

## Core methodological rule

> **Do not ask the skeptic to accept Christianity in order to prove Christianity.**

Start with premises the skeptic can investigate independently.

Then build the case one conclusion at a time.
