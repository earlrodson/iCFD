# The Historical Case for the Resurrection of Jesus

## A Historical and Philosophical Apologetics Framework

### Purpose

This document presents a cumulative case for the Resurrection of Jesus using historical reasoning first, then philosophical reasoning.

The method is:

> Historical data -> source evaluation -> historical facts -> competing explanations -> best explanation -> philosophical implications

The argument does not begin by assuming that the Bible is inspired, that Christianity is true, or that miracles are possible.

---

# 1. The Historical Question

The question is not:

> "Can we prove the Resurrection in a laboratory?"

The Resurrection is a unique historical claim. Historical events are investigated through testimony, documents, archaeology, chronology, contextual evidence, and explanatory reasoning.

The proper question is:

> **What explanation best accounts for the historical evidence concerning Jesus after his crucifixion?**

The Christian answer is:

> **Jesus was bodily raised from the dead by God.**

---

# 2. Establish the Historical Ground First

A useful progression is:

1. Jesus existed.
2. Jesus was crucified.
3. Jesus died.
4. Jesus was buried.
5. His followers very early believed that he appeared to them alive.
6. Paul, formerly opposed to the Christian movement, became a Christian and missionary.
7. James, Jesus' brother, became a leader of the Christian movement.
8. Resurrection proclamation appeared extremely early.
9. The empty tomb is historically defensible, although more contested than the preceding points.
10. The Resurrection provides the best overall explanation of the evidence.

---

# 3. Jesus Existed

Jesus' existence is not dependent solely on the New Testament.

## Josephus

Flavius Josephus, a first-century Jewish historian, refers to Jesus and to James, the brother of Jesus.

The longer passage in *Antiquities* 18 contains later Christian textual additions, but most scholars accept that Josephus originally contained a reference to Jesus even though the exact original wording is debated.

The reference to James in *Antiquities* 20 is especially important because it identifies James as the brother of Jesus who was called Christ. This passage is generally regarded as authentic.

## Tacitus

Tacitus, a Roman historian writing around AD 115-117, reports that:

- Christians took their name from Christus;
- Christus was executed during the reign of Tiberius;
- the execution occurred under Pontius Pilate;
- the movement subsequently spread.

Tacitus was not a Christian source and was hostile toward Christianity.

## Pliny the Younger

Writing around AD 111-113, Pliny describes Christians worshipping Christ and gathering for religious purposes.

Pliny does not provide an independent biography of Jesus, but he demonstrates that within roughly eighty years of Jesus' death there was an established movement centered on Christ.

---

# 4. Jesus Was Crucified

The crucifixion is one of the strongest established facts concerning Jesus.

It is supported by:

- Paul's letters;
- the Gospel traditions;
- Josephus;
- Tacitus;
- the broader Roman historical context.

The crucifixion is accepted across a very broad range of scholarship, including non-Christian scholarship.

This matters because the Resurrection claim is not:

> "Jesus disappeared."

It is:

> **A man known to have been executed was subsequently proclaimed by his followers as risen from the dead.**

---

# 5. Paul's Letters: Extremely Early Evidence

Paul's undisputed letters are generally dated to approximately the AD 50s.

Jesus' death is commonly placed around AD 30.

That means some of Paul's letters were written approximately twenty years after Jesus' death.

For ancient history, this is remarkably close.

More importantly, Paul personally knew members of the first generation of Jesus' followers.

## Galatians 1:18-19

Paul says that after his conversion he went to Jerusalem and met:

- Cephas/Peter;
- James, the brother of Jesus.

The chain is:

> Jesus -> Peter and James -> Paul -> Paul's letters

Paul is therefore not separated from Jesus' earliest movement by centuries.

---

# 6. 1 Corinthians 15 and the Early Resurrection Tradition

One of the most important pieces of evidence is 1 Corinthians 15:3-8.

Paul writes:

> "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received..."

He then gives the tradition:

> Christ died  
> Christ was buried  
> Christ was raised  
> Christ appeared to Cephas  
> Christ appeared to the Twelve  
> Christ appeared to James  
> Christ appeared to Paul

The wording strongly indicates that Paul is passing on a tradition he himself had received.

The tradition therefore predates the letter.

Many historical scholars consider this tradition substantially earlier than 1 Corinthians itself. Some date its formation to only a few years after Jesus' death, although the exact dating is debated.

### Why this matters

The simple theory:

> "The Resurrection was invented generations later"

does not fit the chronology.

The Resurrection proclamation appears extremely early.

---

# 7. Paul Was Not Initially a Christian

Paul is especially important because he did not begin as a Christian missionary.

According to Paul's own letters, he had actively opposed the Christian movement.

Yet he later became one of its principal missionaries.

He attributes this transformation to an encounter with the risen Christ.

This gives historians a significant question:

> **What caused Paul's conversion?**

Possible explanations include:

- a vision;
- hallucination;
- psychological experience;
- religious experience;
- mistaken interpretation;
- deliberate fabrication;
- an actual encounter with the risen Jesus.

The point is not that Paul's explanation must automatically be accepted.

The point is:

> **The conversion itself requires historical explanation.**

---

# 8. James, the Brother of Jesus

James provides another important piece of evidence.

Paul identifies James as the Lord's brother and includes James among those to whom the risen Jesus appeared.

James subsequently became an important leader in the Jerusalem Christian movement.

Josephus independently refers to the execution of James, identifying him as the brother of Jesus who was called Christ.

Therefore we have:

> Paul -> James was an important Christian leader and associated his conversion with a resurrection appearance.

> Josephus -> James, brother of Jesus called Christ, was executed.

The Christian explanation is:

> James encountered the risen Jesus.

A skeptic may propose that James had a visionary or psychological experience. That possibility should be considered.

But the historical question remains:

> **What best explains James' transformation?**

---

# 9. The Disciples' Belief

Distinguish:

### Claim A

The disciples believed Jesus appeared to them.

### Claim B

Jesus actually rose.

Claim A is a historical question.

Claim B is the explanation of Claim A.

The historical evidence strongly supports the proposition that the earliest Christians genuinely believed Jesus had risen.

This belief was not a late theological development. It was foundational to the earliest Christian proclamation.

---

# 10. The Empty Tomb

The empty tomb deserves separate treatment because it is more contested among historians than the crucifixion or the disciples' belief.

A strong case can nevertheless be made.

## Evidence commonly presented

### 1. The burial tradition

The Gospels place Jesus' burial in a specific tomb associated with Joseph of Arimathea.

### 2. The Jerusalem setting

The Christian movement began publicly in Jerusalem, the same broad location where Jesus had been executed.

### 3. Early Resurrection proclamation

The earliest proclamation was that Jesus had been raised.

### 4. Women as the discoverers

The Gospel accounts identify women as the first discoverers of the empty tomb.

This is historically interesting because women occupied a lower status as legal witnesses in many first-century Jewish contexts.

This does not prove the account is true, but it is compatible with the historical criterion often called embarrassment.

### 5. Counter-explanations

Matthew records an early accusation that the disciples stole the body.

Whether or not Matthew's account is accepted as historical, the tradition demonstrates that an empty-tomb explanation involving the disciples existed early in Christian polemic.

## Important qualification

Do not say:

> "Every historian agrees that the tomb was empty."

A careful argument says:

> **The empty tomb is historically defensible and, when combined with the early resurrection proclamation and other evidence, strengthens the Resurrection case. But it is more contested than the crucifixion, Jesus' existence, or the disciples' resurrection belief.**

---

# 11. Historical Facts in Summary

## Very strongly established

1. Jesus existed.
2. Jesus was crucified under Pontius Pilate.
3. Jesus died.
4. The earliest Christians proclaimed that Jesus had been raised.
5. Jesus' followers sincerely believed that he appeared to them.
6. Paul became a Christian after previously opposing Christianity.
7. James became an important Christian leader.
8. Paul knew Peter and James.

## Strong but more debated

9. Jesus was buried in a known tomb.
10. The tomb was found empty.

This distinction prevents overstatement.

---

# 12. What Needs Explaining?

The historical evidence creates an explanatory problem.

We need to account for:

- Jesus' death;
- the burial tradition;
- the disciples' belief in appearances;
- the extremely early Resurrection proclamation;
- Paul's conversion;
- James' conversion;
- the empty tomb, if accepted.

The question is:

> **Which hypothesis explains the largest number of these facts with the fewest additional assumptions?**

This is an inference to the best explanation.

---

# 13. Alternative Explanation: Deliberate Fraud

The disciples may have knowingly invented the Resurrection.

## What it explains

It could explain why the Resurrection was proclaimed.

## Problems

It has difficulty explaining:

- the apparent sincerity of the disciples;
- Paul's conversion;
- James' conversion;
- why Resurrection belief appeared so early;
- the empty tomb, if accepted.

A person can certainly die for a false belief.

Therefore martyrdom does NOT prove the Resurrection.

What martyrdom can establish is narrower:

> **A person who knowingly dies rather than retracting a claim may be demonstrating sincerity.**

That establishes sincerity, not truth.

The evidence for the exact manner of death of each apostle also varies considerably. Claims about every apostle being martyred should therefore be avoided unless individually documented.

---

# 14. Alternative Explanation: Legend

Perhaps the Resurrection developed as a legend over decades or centuries.

## Problem: chronology

The Resurrection proclamation is already present in Paul's letters around the AD 50s.

The tradition in 1 Corinthians 15 is earlier still.

Paul personally knew Peter and James.

Therefore the theory cannot simply say:

> "The Resurrection story appeared hundreds of years later."

It must explain why the central Resurrection proclamation appears within the first generation.

---

# 15. Alternative Explanation: Hallucination or Vision

This is one of the strongest naturalistic alternatives and should be taken seriously.

Human beings can experience:

- grief visions;
- hallucinations;
- religious experiences;
- altered states;
- powerful psychological experiences.

A vision could potentially explain why an individual believed Jesus appeared to him.

## Problems

A complete hallucination theory must explain:

- Peter;
- the Twelve;
- James;
- Paul;
- the development of bodily Resurrection belief;
- the empty tomb, if accepted.

It also has to explain why first-century Jewish followers interpreted these experiences as bodily Resurrection.

First-century Judaism already had beliefs about resurrection, but normally expected resurrection in connection with the end of the age.

The Christian claim was unusual:

> one individual had already been resurrected in advance of the general resurrection.

Therefore the historian must explain not only the experiences but also the specific interpretation.

---

# 16. Alternative Explanation: Wrong Tomb

Perhaps the disciples went to the wrong tomb.

This could explain:

> "The tomb was empty."

But it does not explain:

- Resurrection appearances;
- Paul's conversion;
- James' conversion;
- the early Resurrection proclamation.

It therefore requires additional explanations.

---

# 17. Alternative Explanation: Body Theft

Perhaps someone stole the body.

Possible suspects include:

- disciples;
- authorities;
- grave robbers;
- unknown persons.

Body theft might explain an empty tomb.

But it does not explain why:

- Peter believed Jesus appeared;
- James converted;
- Paul converted;
- the earliest Christians proclaimed Resurrection.

It therefore solves only part of the historical problem.

---

# 18. Alternative Explanation: Jesus Survived

The "swoon" theory proposes that Jesus did not actually die.

## Problems

Roman crucifixion was an execution method designed to kill.

If Jesus survived, he would likely have been severely:

- injured;
- weakened;
- traumatized;
- physically damaged.

It is difficult to explain why his followers would interpret a barely surviving Jesus as the glorious resurrected Lord.

The Christian proclamation is not:

> "Jesus survived."

It is:

> **"God raised Jesus from the dead."**

The survival hypothesis therefore faces both historical and explanatory difficulties.

---

# 19. Combined Naturalistic Explanations

A critic can combine explanations:

> The disciples had visions + somebody moved the body + the story developed + Paul had a religious experience + James had a religious experience.

This is logically possible.

But it becomes a compound explanation.

Each additional hypothesis has to be independently justified.

The Resurrection hypothesis has an advantage in explanatory simplicity if it can account for the major facts through one central event:

> **God raised Jesus bodily from death.**

The question is not whether a naturalistic combination is imaginable.

The question is:

> **Which explanation has the greatest overall explanatory power and least ad hoc complexity?**

---

# 20. The Role of the Scriptures

The New Testament should be treated as historical evidence, but not circularly.

Do not argue:

> The Bible is God's Word, therefore the Bible is true, therefore Jesus rose.

Instead:

> The New Testament contains ancient documents that can be investigated historically.

Then ask:

- When were they written?
- Who wrote them?
- What sources did they use?
- How close were they to the events?
- Did the authors have access to eyewitnesses?
- Are there multiple traditions?
- Does archaeology support the historical setting?
- How were the manuscripts transmitted?
- What do non-Christian sources say?

Only after establishing the historical credibility of the documents should their theological claims be evaluated.

---

# 21. Manuscript Reliability

Two questions must be separated.

## Question A

What did the original authors write?

## Question B

Did later manuscripts preserve those writings accurately?

Textual criticism addresses Question B.

The New Testament has a large manuscript tradition consisting of:

- Greek manuscripts;
- early papyri;
- ancient translations;
- quotations by early Christian writers;
- major codices.

Important early witnesses include:

- Codex Vaticanus;
- Codex Sinaiticus;
- early papyri.

There are textual variants.

That should not be hidden.

But the existence of variants is not equivalent to:

> "We cannot know what the New Testament originally said."

Comparison among manuscripts allows scholars to identify many variants and reconstruct the text with high confidence in most places.

---

# 22. Archaeological Evidence

Archaeology generally cannot prove:

> "Jesus was resurrected."

Archaeology is much better at confirming the historical world in which the events occurred.

Relevant evidence concerns:

- Pontius Pilate;
- first-century Jerusalem;
- Nazareth;
- Capernaum;
- Jewish burial customs;
- Roman crucifixion;
- first-century Judean society.

## Pontius Pilate

The Pilate Stone discovered at Caesarea identifies Pontius Pilate and confirms his historical office and association with Tiberius.

This independently confirms a key figure in the Gospel narrative.

## Nazareth

Archaeology supports the existence of a first-century settlement at Nazareth.

This does not prove every Gospel claim, but it undermines the idea that Nazareth was an invented place.

## Crucifixion

Archaeological evidence associated with Yehohanan demonstrates that Roman crucifixion of Jewish inhabitants occurred in first-century Judea.

Again, archaeology confirms historical context rather than directly proving the Resurrection.

---

# 23. Historical Reasoning Does Not Require Direct Observation

We do not demand a video for ancient history.

For example:

> Caesar crossed the Rubicon.

We cannot reproduce the event.

We infer it from historical testimony and surrounding evidence.

Likewise:

> Jesus rose from the dead.

The question is whether the available evidence makes that explanation rationally preferable.

The uniqueness of the event makes the evidence requirements higher, but not impossible in principle.

---

# 24. The Philosophical Problem of Miracles

A skeptic may say:

> "Miracles are impossible."

Ask:

> **Why?**

If the answer is:

> "Because miracles do not happen."

that is circular.

If the claim is:

> "Nature is causally closed."

then that is a metaphysical claim, not a historical observation.

It must itself be defended.

If God exists, then a supernatural event is not automatically impossible.

The real question is:

> **Given all the evidence, is there sufficient reason to believe that God acted in this unique case?**

---

# 25. Bayesian Reasoning

Let:

- R = Jesus was resurrected.
- E = historical evidence.
- G = God exists.

We are interested in:

> P(R | E)

But the evaluation depends on background assumptions.

If someone assumes:

> P(R) = 0

because miracles are impossible, then no historical evidence can change the conclusion.

That is not a neutral evaluation.

A more complete model considers:

> P(R | G)

and

> P(R | not-G)

If God exists, the prior probability of a Resurrection is not the same as it would be under strict naturalism.

This is why historical apologetics and natural theology can reinforce each other.

### Premise 1

Jesus of Nazareth existed.

### Premise 2

Jesus was crucified and died.

### Premise 3

Very shortly afterward, his followers sincerely believed that he had appeared to them alive.

### Premise 4

The Resurrection proclamation was extremely early and can be traced to the first generation.

### Premise 5

Paul, who had opposed Christianity, became a missionary after what he described as an encounter with the risen Jesus.

This is an inference to the best explanation, not a mathematical proof.

# 29. Common Objections

## "The Bible is biased."

Response:

Bias does not automatically make a source useless. Ancient historians are evaluated critically even when they have agendas.

The question is:

> What historical information can this source reliably provide?

## "The Gospels were written too late."

Response:

The undisputed Pauline letters are earlier, and 1 Corinthians 15 contains a tradition that predates Paul's letter.

## "There are manuscript variants."

Response:

Yes. Textual criticism exists precisely because manuscripts differ. Variants do not automatically make the original text unknowable.

## "People die for false beliefs."

Response:

Correct. Martyrdom demonstrates sincerity more readily than truth.

## "Miracles violate science."

Response:

Science describes regular natural processes. The question of whether God can act beyond those processes is partly metaphysical.

## "Hallucinations explain the appearances."

Response:

They may explain some experiences, but a complete theory must account for Peter, James, Paul, the early Resurrection proclamation, the specific Jewish Resurrection interpretation, and the empty tomb if accepted.

# 30. Debate Strategy

When speaking with an atheist, move step by step.

### Stage 1

> "Do you agree Jesus existed?"

### Stage 2

> "Do you agree he was crucified?"

### Stage 3

> "Do you agree his earliest followers believed they encountered him alive afterward?"

### Stage 4

> "Do you agree Paul converted after claiming an encounter with Jesus?"

### Stage 5

> "Do you agree James became a Christian leader?"

### Stage 6

> "What explanation accounts for all of those facts?"

Only then debate:

> "Was the Resurrection the best explanation?"

This keeps the debate focused.

# 31. Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **"The Bible says Jesus rose, therefore Jesus rose."**
   - Circular.

2. **"The apostles died, therefore Christianity is true."**
   - Too strong.

3. **"Everyone agrees the tomb was empty."**
   - False.

4. **"There are no contradictions in the Bible."**
   - Unnecessary for establishing the Resurrection.

5. **"There are thousands of manuscripts, therefore the Resurrection is true."**
   - Manuscript quantity establishes textual transmission, not the truth of the event.

6. **"Archaeology proves Christianity."**
   - Too broad. Archaeology mainly confirms historical context and specific claims.

7. **"Science proves the Resurrection."**
   - Science is not the direct method for testing a unique ancient event.

8. **"Every apostle definitely died as a martyr."**
   - The evidence varies by apostle.

# 33. Bottom Line

The strongest historical case is not:

> **"The Bible says Jesus rose."**

It is:

> **Jesus unquestionably existed and was crucified. His earliest followers very soon proclaimed that he had risen and sincerely believed that they encountered him alive. Paul, formerly an opponent, converted and personally knew Peter and James. James became a leader of the movement. The Resurrection proclamation can be traced to the earliest generation. The empty tomb is historically defensible, though more debated. Naturalistic alternatives explain portions of the evidence but face significant difficulties when required to explain the entire combination. If God exists, a Resurrection is not metaphysically impossible. Therefore the bodily Resurrection of Jesus can be defended as the best overall explanation of the historical evidence.**

The next stage is:

> **If Jesus truly rose from the dead, why should we believe what Jesus taught about God, revelation, and the Church?**

That leads naturally into the historical case for **Jesus' divine identity and the foundation of the Catholic Church**.

The historical argument should distinguish:

- what is nearly universally accepted;
- what is strongly supported;
- what is contested;
- what is theological interpretation.

Doing this makes the apologetic stronger, not weaker.

The goal is not to pretend there are no objections.

The goal is to show that the objections themselves must explain the evidence at least as well as the Resurrection hypothesis.
